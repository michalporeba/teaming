## The guiding idea

AI doesn't remove the need for team programming. It moves the emphasis from producing code together to jointly understanding the problem, choosing solutions, directing the implementation and evaluating the evidence.

The workshops keep a progressive structure, but change what becomes difficult at each step:

1. Coordinating people.
2. Directing AI together while keeping understanding.
3. Sustaining collaboration under pressure.
4. Owning decisions under uncertainty.
5. Coordinating several teams without centralising their thinking.

They should help participants move beyond depending on whoever is at the keyboard, and then beyond depending on a particular team lead.

> If the person at the keyboard, the AI conversation, or the lead disappeared now, what would the rest of us still understand and be able to change?

## Principles

- Build shared ownership of the problem, not just shared access to generated code.
- Make participation, understanding and decision-making visible.
- Use test-first development and small feedback loops to explore behavior.
- Keep agreed requirements apart from assumptions and generated suggestions.
- Let people experience contrasting approaches before discussing them.
- Stay kind, considerate and respectful during competition and disruption.
- Treat AI as a tool for implementation and investigation, not the owner of decisions.
- Judge success by the team's ability to explain, challenge, change and hand over its work — not merely by finishing a challenge.

Passing tests are useful evidence, but they don't prove the team chose the right behavior or considered every important requirement.

> Reviewing generated output does not necessarily reveal options or requirements that were never considered.

## The programme

| | Workshop | AI | Length |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | [Foundations of team programming](/workshops/foundations/) | None, including autocomplete | 3 hours |
| 2 | [Team programming with one shared AI](/workshops/shared-ai/) | One shared coding agent | 3 hours |
| 3 | [Collaborative delivery under pressure](/workshops/under-pressure/) | One shared agent per team | 3–4 hours |
| 4 | [Team problem-solving masterclass](/workshops/masterclass/) | Shared agent; humans own the evidence | Half day |
| 5 | [Leading collaborative delivery](/workshops/leading/) | Shared agent per team | Half day |

Lengths include discussion and breaks, and are starting points for pilots. The numbers are learning contexts, not a ranking of people or job titles. The masterclass should welcome business analysts and other domain-oriented people, and the fifth workshop develops leadership rather than presenting a harder coding problem.

Two more workshops complement the sequence without extending it: [Inherit and change](/workshops/inherit-and-change/) and [Choosing how to collaborate](/workshops/choosing-how-to-collaborate/).

## Where it comes from

The original workshops were influenced by Woody Zuill's workshops and *Mob Programming*, coding-dojo practice and the Mob Mentality community. Three of them form the basis of this programme:

- **Introduction** — a single team, often new to team programming, TDD or Git. It became the [foundations workshop](/workshops/foundations/).
- **Fun with team programming** — several teams playing Elevator Saga with the Mob Programming RPG role cards, with participants temporarily removed to see whether context belongs to the team. It became [collaborative delivery under pressure](/workshops/under-pressure/).
- **Problem-solving masterclass** — an unfamiliar language and problem, testing whether collaborative habits survive outside the comfort zone. It became the [masterclass](/workshops/masterclass/), now with requirement elicitation and evaluation of unfamiliar generated work.

The five-step progression, the AI operating agreement, the ownership challenge, the one-shot finale, the game designs and the leadership adaptations are proposals developed for this programme. They aren't published rules from the sources below.

## Where it's going

This site will grow into detailed workshop guides and exercise packs: outcomes, audience and preparation; timings, rotations and interventions; participant briefs and facilitator-only requirements; scenarios, scoring and finale material; debrief questions, observation guides and pilot findings; and guidance for training facilitators.

The [games](/games/) may live in separate repositories, linked from the relevant workshops.

A cyber-dojo-like platform could eventually provide standard environments, exercises, role timers, staged scenarios and game sessions, reducing preparation effort. That is a possible future project only; early pilots use existing tools and runnable starter materials.
