---
title: The cards exercise
lede: A short, no-code opener that contrasts a task you can split with a task that needs shared decisions.
---

Use a deck of icebreaker question cards. Nobody is ever asked to answer the questions.

## Round 1: a separable task

Shuffle the cards and ask the group to sort them alphabetically. Let them choose how to distribute the work; steer towards splitting and merging if needed.

Time it, and ask how confident they are in the result. People usually share a clear sorting rule and trust each other to apply it.

## Round 2: a shared-preference task

Shuffle again and use the same distribution. Each person keeps the questions they would be comfortable answering in a work setting.

Ask how confident they are that everyone would accept every retained card. Pass the retained cards between people as a simulated code review, asking about confidence after each pass.

Then ask whether the discarded pile contains a card someone would have wanted to answer. Reviewing only what was kept can't recover the possibilities that were thrown away.

## Round 3: real-time collaboration

Shuffle and distribute again. Participants read cards aloud and the whole group decides together whether to keep each one.

Discussion and brief attempts to persuade are fine. Nobody has to explain or defend their discomfort. If at least one person still objects, the card goes. Avoid repeatedly pressing anyone to reconsider.

Time it, compare experiences, and discuss what people learned about each other — without answering a single question.

## Debrief

Contrast a clear, separable rule with a task that needs preferences to be discovered and reconciled. Connect this to code review, requirements, shared decisions and the possibilities that were never considered.

Use the timings as discussion material, not as proof that collaboration is always just as fast. Practice, repetition and differences between the tasks all affect the comparison.

The no-explanation veto protects personal comfort in this exercise. It isn't a general rule for engineering decisions.

## Optional AI round

*What can be delegated, and what needs shared understanding?*

Run this as a **facilitator demonstration**, not participant AI use, so it fits the no-AI policy of the [foundations workshop](/workshops/foundations/). It answers the question participants often ask there: *why aren't we using AI?*

1. **A separable task.** Photograph or type the cards into an AI tool and ask it to sort them alphabetically. Compare its time and result with Round 1. How confident are they, and how would they check? A clear rule can be delegated and checked cheaply.
2. **A shared-preference task.** Ask the AI to select the cards *this team* would be comfortable answering at work. Would the group accept the selection? What would the AI need to know to choose well?
3. **Contrast.** Could a longer prompt supply what the AI is missing — and who would decide what goes in it? Compare with Round 3, where the information and the decision were both produced in the room.

The teaching point: AI isn't excluded because it's unhelpful. Delegation works when the rule is clear and the result is cheap to check. When the task depends on preferences and context held by different people, the real work is building shared understanding — and the foundations workshop practises that first, with nothing standing between the people and the code.

Keep the no-explanation rule. Don't stage the demonstration so the AI fails; report what it actually did. If it produces a plausible selection, ask what evidence would show it represents this group rather than an average reader.

In the [shared AI workshop](/workshops/shared-ai/), rerun this round briefly as the opener, with the team operating the AI. Ask what the team must write down, and who must agree, before they would trust it with the second task. This leads into the working agreement and decision record.
